Patch it, rebuild it,
or leave it alone?

A practical way to decide what your website actually needs, starting from the jobs it does for your customers and your team.

By Eren · September 29, 2026 · A general guide, not a diagnosis of your business.

On this page

When a website starts to feel tired, the easy move is to shop for a new one. Sometimes that’s the right call. But a rebuild takes time and money, and a plain site that does its jobs well can be exactly right. This is a way to decide that works whoever does the work, including you.

1. Start with the jobs, not the looks

Write down the three to five things customers come to your site to do. Usually that’s some mix of: see whether you do their kind of job, check where you work, get a feel for price or process, contact you, and book. Then add the jobs the site does for your team, like taking inquiries, answering the same questions so you don’t have to, or passing details into your other tools.

Now go through each job on your phone, the way a customer would. Mark each one: works, works but awkward, doesn’t work, or not possible. That list will make the decision for you more often than the design will.

2. When to leave it alone

If the jobs work, customers find what they need, you can change the things you change often (hours, prices, services, photos) without calling anyone, and nothing important gets typed twice, the site is doing its job. A simple brochure site that does this is fine. Put the time and money toward whatever is actually holding the business back.

3. When a patch is enough

A few jobs are awkward, but the structure is sound. A phone number that doesn’t tap to call. A contact form that lands in an inbox nobody checks. No page for a service you want more of. Hours that disagree between two pages. These fixes are usually specific and quick. If your list of problems is short and each one is concrete, patch it.

4. When a rebuild is justified

A rebuild starts to make sense when the problems are about the foundation, not the details:

  • You can’t make routine changes without help or a fee.
  • The site’s structure no longer matches what you sell or who you sell to.
  • You’re stacking workarounds on top of workarounds.
  • Information gets entered twice between the website and your other tools.
  • You want the site to do real work (quotes, bookings, a customer portal, intake that feeds your job system) and the current platform can’t.

In those cases a new website, sometimes with a proper backend behind it, can be the right answer. A good rebuild starts from the jobs list, keeps what already works, and agrees what “done” means before anything gets built.

One caution: a rebuild won’t fix a problem that isn’t on the website. If too few people find you, or the issue is price or capacity, a new site alone won’t change that.

5. The checklist

  1. Can a customer do each job on their phone in under a minute?
  2. Can you change hours, prices, services and photos yourself?
  3. Are the domain, hosting and site login in your name, and do you know where they are?
  4. What do you pay for the site each month or year, and what does that cover?
  5. Does anything get retyped between the website and your other tools?
  6. What do you want the site to do a year from now that it can’t do today?
  7. Is the problem actually on the website, or is it how many people find you, your pricing or your capacity?

Mostly fine answers: leave it. A few specific failures: patch it. Failures on ownership, changes, retyping or a new goal the platform can’t support: a rebuild is worth scoping.
