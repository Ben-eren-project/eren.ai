Patch it, rebuild it,
or leave it alone?

Decide what your website needs, starting from the jobs it does for customers and your team.

By Eren · September 29, 2026 · A general guide, not a diagnosis of your business.

On this page
01 / Start with the jobs02 / Leave it alone03 / Patch it04 / Rebuild it05 / The checklist

A tired-looking site tempts you to buy a new one. Sometimes that’s right. But rebuilds cost time and money, and a plain site that does its jobs can be exactly right.

1. Start with the jobs, not the looks

List the three to five things customers visit your site to do: see if you do their kind of job, check where you work, get a feel for price or process, contact you, book. Add what it does for your team: taking inquiries, answering common questions, passing details to other tools.

Try each job on your phone. Mark it: works, works but awkward, doesn’t work, or not possible. That list decides more often than the design does.

2. When to leave it alone

If customers find what they need, you can change hours, prices, services and photos yourself, and nothing important gets typed twice, the site is doing its job. Even a plain brochure site. Spend the money on what’s really holding the business back.

3. When a patch is enough

A few jobs are awkward, but the structure is sound: a number that doesn’t tap to call, a form that lands in an unread inbox, no page for a service you want more of, hours that differ between pages. If the list is short and each problem is concrete, patch it.

4. When a rebuild is justified

Rebuild when the foundation is the problem, not the details:

  • You can’t make routine changes without help or a fee.
  • The structure no longer fits what you sell or who you sell to.
  • You’re stacking workarounds on top of workarounds.
  • Details get entered twice between the site and your other tools.
  • You want the site to do real work (quotes, bookings, a customer portal, intake that feeds your job system) and the current platform can’t.

Then a new site, sometimes with a proper backend, can be right. A good rebuild starts from the jobs list, keeps what works and agrees what “done” means before anything is built.

One caution: a rebuild won’t fix problems that aren’t on the website, like too few people finding you, price or capacity.

5. The checklist

  1. Can a customer do each job on their phone in under a minute?
  2. Can you change hours, prices, services and photos yourself?
  3. Are the domain, hosting and site login in your name, and do you know where?
  4. What does the site cost a month or year, and what does that cover?
  5. Does anything get retyped between the website and your other tools?
  6. What should the site do a year from now that it can’t today?
  7. Is the problem really the website, or how many people find you, your pricing or your capacity?

Mostly fine: leave it. A few specific failures: patch it. Failures on ownership, changes, retyping or a new goal the platform can’t support: scope a rebuild.

If you’re still not sure

Keep the jobs list and checklist answers. They’re useful whether you fix things yourself, hire someone or talk to us.
