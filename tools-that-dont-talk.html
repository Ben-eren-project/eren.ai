Tools that don’t talk
to each other

A half-hour way to find where information gets typed twice in your business, and to decide what’s worth doing about it.

By Eren · October 9, 2026 · A general guide, not a diagnosis of your business.

On this page

You bought each tool for a good reason. One takes bookings. One sends invoices. There’s a spreadsheet the office trusts, and a form on the website. Each one works. The cost, when there is one, shows up in between: the same customer typed in again, somewhere else, by a person who had other things to do.

This is a way to find out whether that’s happening in your business, and what to do about it. It takes about half an hour and a pen. It works whoever ends up doing the fixing, including you.

1. Follow one real customer

Pick a job you finished recently. Not your best one or your worst one. An ordinary one.

Write down every place that customer’s details were entered, from first contact to final payment. For each place, note three things:

  • Where it was entered: the website form, a text, the scheduling tool, the invoice, a notebook.
  • Who entered it.
  • Where they got it from: the customer directly, or another one of your tools.

Don’t tidy it up. If the address was read off a text message and typed into the schedule, write that down exactly.

2. Mark the retypes

Go down your list and mark every line where the answer to “where did they get it from” is another tool, a piece of paper, or someone’s memory. Those are your retypes: information you already had, entered again by hand.

Count them for that one job. That number is yours and it’s real, which makes it more useful than any average you’ll read online.

Then do one honest check. Did any retype go wrong on that job? A wrong address, an old price, a note that never reached the person doing the work. Mark those twice.

3. Ask four questions, in this order

Take each retype and ask:

  1. Does a tool I already pay for do this? Look in the settings and the help pages of the tool on the receiving end. It may have a feature you never switched on, and that costs nothing extra.
  2. Can these two tools connect? Check both vendors’ lists of connections, on the plan you actually have. Until you’ve checked both, a connection is a hope and not a plan.
  3. Are two tools doing the same job? If your scheduling tool can also send the reminder, you may not need the separate reminder app.
  4. Is this one fine as it is? A two-minute retype that happens once a week, and never goes wrong, may not be worth touching.

Stop at the first question that gets a yes. The order matters: it runs from the cheapest fix to the most expensive one.

4. Configure, connect, replace, or leave

Configure when the tool you have already does it. This is the cheapest fix and the one people skip, because it feels too small to be the answer.

Connect when both tools support it and the retype happens on most jobs. Remember a connection is one more thing that can break. Know who will notice when it does.

Replace when a tool can’t do a job you now need, or when two tools overlap and one of them clearly does both jobs. Count the cost of moving your records and of everyone learning something new, not only the subscription.

Leave it when the retype is rare, quick and reliable. Not every gap needs closing.

If none of your tools can do what the business now needs, a custom tool or a rebuilt website with a proper backend can be the right answer. It should be the last thing you reach for, after you’ve checked what you already own.

5. The checklist

  1. For one ordinary job, how many times were the customer’s details entered by hand?
  2. Which of those were copied from another tool you already have?
  3. Did any copy go wrong on that job?
  4. For each tool: who holds the login, and is the account in the business’s name?
  5. What does each tool cost a month, and which features on that plan are you not using?
  6. Can you export your customer list and job history from each one?
  7. If one tool stopped working tomorrow, what would stop with it?

Few retypes and none going wrong: leave it. A feature you already own would cover it: configure. The same retype on most jobs and both tools support a connection: connect. Two tools for one job, or a need none of them meets: replacing or building is worth scoping.

If you’re still not sure

Keep the list from step 1. One real job, written down honestly, is the most useful thing you can hand to anyone who offers to help, and it stops you buying a fix for a problem you don’t have.
