Tools that don’t talk
to each other

Find where information gets typed twice in your business, and decide what’s worth fixing.

By Eren · October 9, 2026 · A general guide, not a diagnosis of your business.

On this page
01 / Follow one customer02 / Mark the retypes03 / Four questions04 / Configure, connect, replace, leave05 / The checklist

Your booking tool, invoices, office spreadsheet and website form each work. The cost, when there is one, sits in between: the same customer typed in again by someone with other things to do.

Finding it takes about half an hour and a pen.

1. Follow one real customer

Pick an ordinary job you finished recently, not your best or worst.

Write down every place that customer’s details were entered, from first contact to final payment. For each, note:

  • Where it was entered: the website form, a text, the scheduling tool, the invoice, a notebook.
  • Who entered it.
  • Where they got it from: the customer, or another of your tools.

Don’t tidy it up. If the address was read off a text and typed into the schedule, write exactly that.

2. Mark the retypes

Mark every line where “where they got it from” is another tool, paper or someone’s memory. Those are your retypes: information you already had, entered again by hand.

Count them. Your real number beats any average you’ll read online.

Did any retype go wrong? A wrong address, an old price, a note that never reached the person doing the work. Mark those twice.

3. Ask four questions, in this order

Take each retype and ask:

  1. Does a tool I already pay for do this? Check the receiving tool’s settings and help pages for a feature you never switched on. It costs nothing extra.
  2. Can these two tools connect? Check both vendors’ connection lists for the plan you have. Until you’ve checked both, it’s a hope, not a plan.
  3. Are two tools doing the same job? If your scheduler can also send the reminder, you may not need the reminder app.
  4. Is this one fine as it is? A two-minute weekly retype that never goes wrong may not be worth touching.

Stop at the first yes. The order runs from cheapest fix to most expensive.

4. Configure, connect, replace, or leave

Configure when a tool you have already does it. It’s the cheapest fix, and the most often skipped because it feels too small.

Connect when both tools support it and the retype happens on most jobs. Connections can break, so know who will notice.

Replace when a tool can’t do a job you now need, or two tools overlap and one does both. Count the cost of moving records and retraining, not only the subscription.

Leave it when the retype is rare, quick and reliable. Not every gap needs closing.

If no tool does what you now need, a custom tool or a rebuilt website with a proper backend can be right. Reach for that last.

5. The checklist

  1. For one ordinary job, how many times were the customer’s details entered by hand?
  2. Which of those were copied from another tool you already have?
  3. Did any copy go wrong on that job?
  4. For each tool: who holds the login, and is the account in the business’s name?
  5. What does each tool cost a month, and which plan features don’t you use?
  6. Can you export your customer list and job history from each one?
  7. If one tool stopped working tomorrow, what would stop with it?

Few retypes, none going wrong: leave it. A feature you own covers it: configure. Same retype on most jobs, both tools support it: connect. Two tools for one job, or a need none meets: scope a replacement or build.

If you’re still not sure

Keep the list from step 1. It’s the best thing to hand anyone offering help, and it stops you buying a fix for a problem you don’t have.
