Eren field notes / Website & inquiries

Before you rebuild your site,
follow one inquiry.

A practical checklist for business owners: find the unclear step before deciding whether the answer is a new page, a better handoff or a different process.

By Eren · September 22, 2026 · A general checklist, not a diagnosis of your business.

A website can look dated and still work for its customers. A polished one can leave people unsure what to do next. Appearance alone tells you little about where an inquiry gets stuck. Start by walking the same path a customer would take.

1. Can a customer recognize the right service?

Open the page a customer is likely to land on, using your phone. Can you tell what the business does, who the service is for, and whether it serves the customer’s needs? Read the page without using what you already know as the owner.

Look for: a service description that answers a real buying question. If customers repeatedly ask whether you offer something that is already listed, that is a reason to investigate how it is explained. It is not proof that a redesign will increase sales.

2. Is the next step clear and usable?

Follow the main contact, quote or booking link. Check whether it reaches the intended destination on mobile. Look at what information the customer is asked to provide and whether it is clear what happens after they respond.

A phone-first route may suit your business. An online form may suit another. Choose based on customer needs, accessibility and your ability to respond—not a rule that every site must have the same features.

3. Does the inquiry reach a person who owns it?

With the owner’s permission, submit a clearly labeled test through your own form. Confirm both sides: what the visitor sees, and what arrives in the business’s inbox or system. A success message alone does not prove delivery. A missing success message alone does not prove the inquiry was lost.

Record the test time, destination, required information and responsible person. If the website hands off to a separate booking tool, check that transition too. Avoid using real customer details in a test.

4. What happens after the first reply?

Trace an inquiry through the next decision: qualified, quoted, booked, declined or still waiting. Is there a named owner and an agreed next action? Ask the person doing the work where they have to remember, retype or chase something.

Slow follow-up is a hypothesis until you have records or an owner’s account to support it. Don’t assume a fixed number of messages is right for every business. A useful process respects the customer’s choice to stop.

5. Decide what would show the change worked.

Separate a delivery check from a business result. “The form delivers a labeled test to the right inbox” verifies a fix. “More qualified inquiries become bookings” needs reliable before-and-after records and enough time to observe what happened.

  • For a broken link: verify the destination on phone and desktop.
  • For a handoff: verify receipt, required details and a responsible person.
  • For a follow-up process: record whether the agreed next action happened.
  • For a business outcome: compare the same definition over stated periods, noting changes in demand, pricing or capacity.

When records are missing, begin recording them. Don’t replace an unknown with a guessed conversion rate.

Keep what works.

If the path is clear, inquiries arrive and the business can handle them, the right decision may be to leave the website alone. Use the evidence to choose a change, not to justify one already sold.
