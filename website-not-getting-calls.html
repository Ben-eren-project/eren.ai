Why isn’t my website
getting calls?

Five checks you can run from your own phone in about twenty minutes, before you pay for a redesign or more ads.

By Eren · September 27, 2026 · A general guide, not a diagnosis of your business.

On this page

When the phone goes quiet, the usual reaction is to blame the website’s look and start shopping for a new one. Sometimes that’s right. Often the site is fine and one small step between “interested” and “calling” is broken. These checks help you find which it is. Do them on your phone, since that’s where many local customers will meet you.

1. Can someone call you with one tap?

Open your site on your phone and look at the first screen. Is your phone number there, and does tapping it start a call? A number shown as an image, or plain text that isn’t a link, makes the customer copy it by hand. Some won’t bother.

Look for: a tap-to-call link on the home page and on every service page, not only on the contact page.

2. Does the page say what you actually do?

Read your home page as a stranger would. Within a few seconds, can you tell what the business does, what kind of job it takes on, and who it’s for? “Quality service since 1998” doesn’t tell someone with a leaking water heater that you fix water heaters.

Look for: the services people call about, named in the words they’d use, near the top of the page.

3. Does it say where you work?

A customer in the next town over wants to know if you’ll come to them before they call. If your site never names the towns or area you serve, they have to guess. It also makes it harder for search engines to connect you with nearby searches.

Look for: a plain line or short list of the towns you cover, in text rather than only on a map image.

4. Does the contact form actually reach a person?

Fill out your own form with a test message and note the time. Did it arrive? In which inbox? Who saw it, and when? Forms that were set up years ago often send to an old address or a folder nobody checks. The site looks like it works, but the message goes nowhere useful.

Look for: a test inquiry that lands in an inbox someone reads every day, and a reply within the time you’d want as a customer.

5. What happens after hours?

Call your own business line at 8 pm. What does the caller hear? Is there a voicemail, and does anyone listen to it first thing? If most of your calls come while you’re on a job or after closing, this is often where work slips away quietly.

Look for: a clear message that says when they’ll hear back, and a habit (or a simple tool) that makes sure they do.

If all five check out

Then the website may not be the problem at all. The issue might be how many people find you in the first place, what you charge, or how busy you already are. That’s worth knowing before spending money on a rebuild.
